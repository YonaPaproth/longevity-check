## What is L-Citrulline?

L-citrulline is a non-proteinogenic amino acid naturally found in watermelon and other cucurbits. In the body, L-citrulline is converted to **L-arginine** in the kidneys—and L-arginine is in turn the critical substrate for the synthesis of **nitric oxide (NO)** in blood vessels.

This detour through citrulline is decisive: Directly ingested L-arginine is heavily metabolized in the gut and liver (first-pass effect), so little reaches the bloodstream. L-citrulline, by contrast, largely bypasses this degradation and raises arginine blood levels more effectively than equivalent doses of L-arginine itself.

**NO** causes relaxation of vascular smooth muscle (vasodilation) and can thereby improve blood flow—a mechanism relevant both for muscle perfusion during exertion and for blood pressure regulation.

## What Do the Studies Actually Show?

### Positive Findings
- Multiple RCTs demonstrate that **6–8 g citrulline malate** before training can boost performance during high-intensity and endurance efforts—such as more repetitions, lower perceived fatigue.
- Some studies suggest modest **blood pressure benefits** in people with mildly elevated baseline values.
- L-citrulline reliably raises L-arginine plasma levels more effectively than L-arginine supplementation alone.

### Limitations
- Many studies use **citrulline malate** (citrulline bound to malic acid), not pure L-citrulline. The separate contribution of malic acid is not fully established.
- Effect sizes are moderate; reproducibility across different laboratories is inconsistent.
- Studies are often **brief** (acute single-dose tests) with small sample sizes. Multi-week or multi-month studies of long-term effects are rare.
- Robust long-term data on blood pressure or cardiovascular health are lacking.

> **Bottom Line:** The evidence is promising, especially for high-intensity training. However, it is less robust and consistent than with creatine or caffeine.

## Mechanism: Citrulline → Arginine → NO

The nitric oxide system operates through the following steps:

1. **L-Citrulline** is taken orally and reaches the kidneys.
2. In the kidney, citrulline is converted to **L-arginine** via the enzyme **argininosuccinate synthetase** and other urea cycle reactions.
3. L-arginine is the substrate for **eNOS (endothelial nitric oxide synthase)** in blood vessel walls.
4. eNOS produces NO, which relaxes vascular smooth muscle → **vasodilation**.
5. Improved blood flow can support muscles in studies by delivering oxygen and nutrients more rapidly.

## Dosing and Administration

- **For training (used in studies):** 6–8 g citrulline malate or 3–6 g pure L-citrulline, 30–60 minutes before exercise.
- **For blood pressure (in studies):** 3–5 g/day L-citrulline, continuously.
- The equivalence between **citrulline malate and pure L-citrulline** is not linear. Common commercial citrulline malate comes in a 2:1 ratio (citrulline:malic acid by weight).

## Drug and Nutrient Interactions

- **PDE-5 inhibitors** (sildenafil, tadalafil): L-citrulline theoretically potentiates the NO signaling pathway—simultaneous use with these medications is not recommended, as severe blood pressure drops are possible.
- **Antihypertensives**: Additive blood pressure-lowering effects are possible. Consult a physician.
- No known interactions with most common dietary supplements.

## Is L-Citrulline Safe?

L-citrulline is considered **likely safe** at usual doses. Studies report amounts up to 15 g/day without serious adverse effects. Gastrointestinal disturbances (nausea, loose stools) are possible at higher doses. For healthy adults without circulatory problems or concurrent medications, there is no evidence of organ damage.

Certain populations should consult a physician:
- People with known kidney or liver disease (urea cycle involvement)
- Those taking blood pressure medications or other vascular drugs
- People with rare metabolic disorders (e.g., urea cycle defects)

## Regulatory Status in Germany and the EU

- L-citrulline is **not classified as a novel food** in the EU and may be sold as a dietary supplement.
- **Health Claims:** The EFSA has **not approved** any health claims for L-citrulline regarding blood pressure, blood flow, or athletic performance. Marketing claims must remain neutral.
- Citrulline malate is also approved for sale; the combination is subject to no special licensing requirements when declared as a food.

## Conclusion

L-citrulline is an interesting supplement with a plausible mechanism and solid short-term study data. For athletes performing high-intensity efforts who want to optimize endurance or recovery times, it may be a worthwhile supplement. However, clear long-term data and robust dose-response curves are lacking. For general health or longevity goals, the evidence base is limited.
