## What is sulforaphan?

Sulforaphan is an **isothiocyanate** — a sulfur-rich plant compound found in cruciferous vegetables (Brassica family): broccoli, Brussels sprouts, cabbage, kohlrabi. It does not exist directly in the plant but is formed when the precursor molecule **glucoraphanin** comes into contact with the enzyme **myrosinase** during chewing or cutting.

What makes sulforaphan particularly interesting is its mechanism: it activates **Nrf2** (Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2) — a transcription factor that upregulates hundreds of genes for cellular defence and detoxification systems. Nrf2 is considered one of the most important endogenous protection mechanisms against oxidative stress, inflammation, and chemical toxins.

## Nrf2: the master regulator

When sulforaphan activates Nrf2, the cell produces more:
- **Glutathione** (central antioxidant)
- **Heme oxygenase-1** (anti-inflammatory)
- **Phase 2 detoxification enzymes** (detoxification of carcinogens)
- **Thioredoxin** (reduces oxidative stress)

This mechanism distinguishes sulforaphan from direct antioxidants like vitamin C: it induces the body's **own** antioxidant capacity rather than scavenging radicals directly.

## What does the evidence actually show?

### Comparatively well studied
- **Detoxification**: RCTs show measurably increased excretion of aflatoxin and benzene metabolites
- **Inflammatory markers**: CRP and NF-κB activity decline in multiple studies
- **Nrf2 activation**: biomarker studies consistently positive

### Promising
- Cognitive function (schizophrenia, ASD studies)
- Blood pressure reduction
- Glucose metabolism

### Not (yet) established
- Cancer prevention in humans via RCTs (only epidemiological and preclinical data)
- Direct lifespan extension

## The preparation problem

Normal cooking destroys myrosinase — and with it, sulforaphan formation. **Brief steaming (3–4 minutes)** preserves enzyme activity better. Alternatively: sulforaphan supplements from stabilized broccoli sprouts.

Tip: add mustard or horseradish to steamed broccoli — they contain myrosinase and can initiate sulforaphan formation even from cooked broccoli.

## Is sulforaphan safe?

At typical supplement doses (10–50 mg/day), it is considered **likely safe**. At very high doses, possible thyroid issues (isothiocyanates inhibit iodine uptake) — no risk with normal iodine intake. Do not use high doses during pregnancy.
