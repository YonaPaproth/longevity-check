## What is Shilajit?

Shilajit is a dark, tar-like or resinous substance that oozes from rocks in high mountain ranges, particularly in the Himalayas, Andes, and other peaks. It is formed over centuries from the decomposition of plants and organic matter compressed between rock layers. In traditional Ayurvedic medicine, shilajit has been used for centuries as a rejuvenation tonic, marketed under names like "mineral pitch" or "asphaltum punjabianum." Today, it appears in modern supplement marketing as an adaptogen and energy booster, often positioned in performance and longevity stacks alongside other mineral and botanical compounds.

## How It's Marketed

In contemporary supplement culture, shilajit is primarily marketed for three main claims: increasing energy and stamina, supporting testosterone levels and male vitality, and enhancing mitochondrial function. These claims are often bundled with broader narratives about "bioavailable minerals," "fulvic acid delivery," and "cellular energy optimization." The ingredient commands premium prices—often $30–60 per month—and attracts consumers interested in performance enhancement, anti-aging protocols, and traditional medicine approaches.

## What We Know About the Evidence

The human research on shilajit is sparse and methodologically limited. Most available studies are small, conducted in India, and frequently have design limitations such as inadequate placebo controls or unclear randomization methods. The 2016 study by Pandit et al. (PMID: 26395129) evaluated purified shilajit in clinical settings and reported signals of activity, but the sample sizes were modest and the findings cannot be generalized broadly. A 2012 review by Carrasco-Gallardo et al. (PMID: 23717060) noted that shilajit is biochemically interesting in theory—containing compounds like fulvic acid and various minerals—but concluded the evidence remains preliminary.

Most mechanistic studies are conducted in laboratory or animal models, not humans. While test-tube and animal data can be suggestive, they do not reliably predict human effects. The gap between "shilajit is interesting in a petri dish" and "shilajit reliably improves energy in humans" is large and unbridged.

## The Quality Problem

The more pressing concern with shilajit products is quality and safety. As a natural mineral product sourced from remote mountain regions, shilajit is prone to contamination with heavy metals, microbial pathogens, and other impurities. A 2002 review by Ernst (PMID: 12204876) specifically highlighted quality and purity concerns in herbo-mineral products, noting that contamination is particularly common in these categories. Unlike pharmaceutical manufacturing, supplement production is less stringently regulated in most jurisdictions, and testing standards for shilajit products vary widely.

Independent testing of commercial shilajit products has occasionally revealed elevated levels of lead, mercury, or arsenic—heavy metals that accumulate in the body and pose serious health risks with chronic exposure. Because shilajit products often lack third-party verification and are sourced from unregulated suppliers, the risk-benefit calculation shifts dramatically. A contaminated product may do far more harm than any theoretical benefit from the active compounds.

## Practical Takeaway

Shilajit is not an evidence-based compound with a strong basis for supplementation. The human research is limited and low-quality, the mechanistic data is largely preclinical, and the claims made in marketing far exceed what the science supports. More critically, the product quality and contamination risk is a real and tangible concern—one that is not adequately addressed by most commercial suppliers. Even if shilajit's biochemistry were compelling, purchasing an unverified product with unknown purity is a poor risk-benefit trade. For those interested in energy, mitochondrial support, or male performance, other evidence-based approaches (sleep, exercise, CoQ10, carnitine, D-ribose, or specialized minerals) offer more transparent safety and efficacy profiles with stronger human data.
