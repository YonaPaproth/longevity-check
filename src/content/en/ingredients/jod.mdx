## What is iodine?

Iodine is an **essential trace element** required exclusively for synthesizing **thyroid hormones** T3 (triiodothyronine) and T4 (thyroxine). These hormones regulate basal metabolic rate, heat production, heart rate, brain development, and growth.

Approximately 70–80% of total body iodine is stored in the thyroid gland, with the remainder concentrated in breast tissue, ovaries, and gastric mucosa.

## Germany: a structural iodine-deficiency region

Despite operating an iodine salt program, Germany remains classified as an **iodine-deficient region**:

- Median urinary iodine excretion: ~108 µg/L (WHO target: >150 µg/L)
- Root causes: iodine-poor soils, low consumption of seafish, vegan/vegetarian diets without seafood
- High-risk groups: pregnant women (elevated requirements), vegans, people avoiding iodized salt

Iodine deficiency during pregnancy is the most common **preventable cause of intellectual disability** worldwide—even mild hypothyroid states can impair cognition and IQ.

## Iodine and healthy aging

The connection to healthy aging is indirect but biologically plausible:
- Thyroid hormones regulate **mitochondrial biogenesis** and energy metabolism
- Subclinical hypothyroidism (common in iodine deficiency) is associated with elevated cardiovascular risk
- Cognitive function in aging depends on optimal thyroid status

## What does the evidence show?

### Clearly established (EFSA-approved claims)
- Thyroid function: directly essential
- Cognitive development and function
- Normal energy metabolism

### Well-researched
- Iodine deficiency as a cause of goiter (thyroid enlargement)
- Cognitive improvements in iodine-deficient children following supplementation

### Not established
- Anti-aging effects beyond normal requirements
- Additional benefit for adequately nourished individuals

## The dual risk: deficiency AND excess

Iodine demonstrates a classic **U-shaped dose–risk relationship**:

| Iodine Status | Effect |
|---|---|
| Deficiency | Goiter, hypothyroidism, cognitive impairment |
| Optimal (150–250 µg/day) | Normal thyroid function |
| Excess (>500 µg/day chronically) | Can trigger Hashimoto's, hyperthyroidism |

**High-dose iodine supplements** (>500 µg/day) are often counterproductive and frequently marketed in dubious "detox" products.

## Practical guidance

- **Iodized salt**: consistent use is the simplest approach
- **Seafood**: 1–2 times weekly
- **Dairy and dairy products**: good iodine sources (from iodized animal feed)
- **Supplementation**: only if diet is inadequate, then 100–150 µg/day – not more

Have your thyroid function checked with your physician—a simple blood test (TSH) reveals functional status.

## Is iodine safe?

At 150–300 µg/day: **likely safe** for healthy adults. In those with thyroid disease (Hashimoto's, hyperthyroidism, thyroid nodules): supplement only under medical supervision. The therapeutic window is narrow—**caution** with high-dose formulations.
